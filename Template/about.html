Some notes on looking at things.

About

Art is the only field where standing very still in front of something for four minutes counts as participation. Nobody checks whether you understood it. You mainly have to look like you are having a thought.

Most of us are, in fairness. The thought is usually how does that stay up, followed closely by what would that cost. Both are legitimate responses and always have been. The Medici had them too, just in better coats.

There is a persistent rumour that you need some background before any of this is allowed to work on you. You do not. A painting has no idea what you know. It sits there being blue at you whether or not you can name the century, and the blue lands either way — which is either deeply democratic or slightly lazy of the painting, depending on your mood.

The gallery makes it harder than it needs to be. White walls, quiet floors, and a small card carrying the title, the year, and the words mixed media doing an enormous amount of unpaid work. Somewhere behind that card is a person who spent eleven months on this and would very much like to know what you thought. The card will not mention it.

The distance between what somebody meant and what you actually took away is not a fault in the system. It is the system. A photograph made at six in the morning because the light was briefly doing something extraordinary can arrive in your head as a memory of a holiday you never went on, and that is not a mistranslation. That is the second half of the work, and you are the one doing it.

You are also allowed to dislike things. Confidently, out loud, in front of the artist if the moment truly calls for it. Taste is not an exam you can fail, and anyone insisting a piece is important is usually describing a spreadsheet rather than the thing on the wall. Some of the most expensive art ever sold is, on any honest afternoon, a bit boring.

So: a portfolio, which is a gallery you can visit in socks. Everything in it was made on purpose. Some of it took far longer than it looks like it did, and some of it took an afternoon and came out better — a ratio that remains one of the great unsolved problems of the field, with nobody close.

Most of what is here started out as something else entirely. A colour that turned up in the wrong file and then refused to leave. A crop that was only ever meant to be temporary. A layout that worked because of a mistake nobody has since been able to reproduce on purpose. The finished thing looks decided, which is the polite fiction every finished thing tells.

And then it gets collected, which mostly means it ends up on a shelf, acquires a coffee ring, survives three house moves and one determined decluttering phase, and quietly becomes part of why a room looks the way it does. Objects are patient like that. They wait for you to notice them properly, and they never once mention how long it took.

Stay as long as you like. Nobody is timing you here either.
